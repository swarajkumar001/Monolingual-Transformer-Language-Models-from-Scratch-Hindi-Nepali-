The code they open

They walked your crawler/OCR, tokenizer, Transformer and train loop last time. Expect the same, plus reasoning/ this time. Know what each file does in one sentence before you know any line of it.

Walk me through the forward pass.

Do it in tensor shapes, not adjectives. For a batch of B sequences of length T:

idx            (B, T)          token ids
token_embedding(B, T, 512)     nn.Embedding(vocab, 512)
+ position     (T, 512)        broadcast over batch
embedding_dropout               p = 0.1

per block (×6), pre-norm:
  LayerNorm  → W_q/W_k/W_v     (B, T, 512) each
  reshape    → (B, 8, T, 64)   64 = 512/8 = d_k
  QKᵀ/√d_k   → (B, 8, T, T)
  + causal mask (−inf above diagonal)
  softmax    → attn_dropout → @V → (B, 8, T, 64)
  merge      → (B, T, 512) → W_o → resid_dropout
  + residual
  LayerNorm  → 512 → 2048 → GELU → 512 → dropout
  + residual

final LayerNorm
lm_head        (B, T, vocab)   tied to token_embedding
Say this

"Three reshapes matter: (B,T,512) → (B,T,8,64) → (B,8,T,64) so each head attends in its own 64-dim subspace, then the inverse to concatenate before W_o."

Show me the causal mask actually works.

You have a test for exactly this — hindi/tests/test_model.py :: test_causal_mask_blocks_the_future. It perturbs every token after position t and measures the change in logits at positions ≤ t.

Say this

"The measured change is exactly 0.000e+00 — not within tolerance. softmax(−inf) is exactly zero and 0 × v is exactly zero, so the future contributes nothing at all, not just something small."

The mask is an additive upper-triangular matrix of −inf applied before softmax, registered as a buffer and sliced to [:T,:T].

Why 1/√d_k?

Q and K entries are roughly zero-mean unit-variance, so a dot product over d_k = 64 terms has variance ≈ 64 and standard deviation ≈ 8. Feeding numbers of that scale into softmax saturates it: one logit dominates, the distribution goes nearly one-hot, and the gradient through softmax vanishes.

Say this

"Dividing by √d_k returns the scores to unit variance, which keeps softmax in its responsive range. Without it, deeper models simply stop training."

What is in a checkpoint, and does resume actually work?

  • model weights, optimizer state, scheduler state
  • current step, tokens consumed, best validation loss
  • the full run config
  • both RNG states — torch and numpy

Resume was verified across three consecutive sessions before the real runs. The RNG states are the part most people omit: without them a resumed run draws a different data order and is not the same experiment.

Known bug you fixed — own it

The LR schedule originally restored its horizon from the checkpoint. Resuming with a larger max_steps then left step > max_steps, pinning the learning rate at its floor so training looked stalled. Fixed: restore progress only, take the schedule's shape from the live config.

03

Positional encoding, and RoPE

They raised RoPE last time. Be completely clear on what you did and did not build.

The trap

You did not implement RoPE. Your POS_ENCODINGS are "learned", "sinusoidal" and "none". You shipped learned absolute.

Never imply otherwise. The strong answer is knowing what RoPE does and why you chose differently — that reads as a decision. Claiming it reads as a lie the moment they open model/transformer.py.

What positional encoding did you use, and why?

Learned absolute: an nn.Embedding(block_size, n_embd) table, 512 × 512 = 262,144 parameters, added to the token embedding before dropout.

  • Why not sinusoidal: its advantage is extrapolating past the trained length. You train and evaluate at one fixed length, 512, so that advantage never pays.
  • Why learned: the model allocates positional resolution where its data needs it rather than following a fixed frequency ladder.
  • The cost, state it yourself: a hard ceiling. There is no embedding row for position 512, so the model cannot be run on longer sequences without retraining that table. forward() raises if T > block_size.

Why not RoPE?

Explain the mechanism first, then the trade:

RoPE does not add anything to the embeddings. It rotates Q and K inside the attention function by an angle proportional to position. Because a dot product of two rotated vectors depends only on the difference of their angles, the attention score between positions i and j becomes a function of i − j — it is relative, not absolute.

Say this

"RoPE is the better default for a model meant to generalise beyond its context window, and it's free in parameters. I train and evaluate at a single fixed 512, so the property it buys is one I never exercise — and it would have meant changing the attention function itself rather than the embedding layer. Given the brief says this is not an architecture bake-off, I spent the complexity budget elsewhere."

What breaks without positional encoding at all?

Self-attention is permutation-equivariant: shuffle the input tokens and the outputs shuffle identically, with no change in content. The model literally cannot tell राम श्याम से लंबा है from श्याम राम से लंबा है — opposite meanings, identical bag of tokens.

Your code supports pos_encoding: "none" precisely so this can be measured — it is the optional bonus ablation. Be honest: you did not run it.

04

Dropout in training

They asked this. The good answer names all four sites and knows the train/eval distinction.

Where is dropout applied?

Four places, all at p = 0.1:

1 · Embedding dropout
On the summed token + positional embedding, before the first block.
2 · Attention dropout
On the softmax probabilities, after softmax and before multiplying by V.
3 · Residual dropout
On the attention sublayer output, after W_o, before the residual add.
4 · Feed-forward dropout
On the FFN output, after the second linear layer.
The point about site 2 — this is the interesting one

"Dropping attention probabilities randomly severs attention edges, so a head can't stake everything on one key position. It's regularising the routing, not just the features. Note the rows no longer sum to 1 after dropout — that's accepted, and PyTorch's inverted dropout rescales by 1/(1−p) so the expectation is preserved."

Is dropout on during evaluation?

No. model.eval() disables every nn.Dropout, and it is called before validation, before generate(), and inside the attention tooling.

Because PyTorch uses inverted dropout — scaling by 1/(1−p) at training time — nothing needs rescaling at test time.

Likely follow-up

"What would happen if you left it on at eval?" → Perplexity would be inflated and non-deterministic: a different random mask each pass, so the same test set would score differently every time. Every metric in your report depends on eval() having been called.

05

Temperature and repetition

They asked about the relation. You have a clean monotonic result — lead with the table.

What is the relation between temperature and repetition?

Mechanism first: temperature divides the logits before softmax. T → 0 is argmax (greedy); T → ∞ is uniform.

P(token) = softmax(logits / T)

Low T sharpens the distribution, so the highest-probability token keeps winning. Once the model re-enters a state it has already been in, it emits the same continuation — a deterministic loop it cannot escape, because nothing is random.

Model H · 64 held-out prompts · the relation is monotonic

Say this

"Greedy decoding degenerates — 59.8% of generated 4-grams are repeats and the samples visibly loop. Raising temperature monotonically destroys that repetition, down to 0.2% at T = 1.0. Model L behaves identically: 0.577 → 0.001."

So which setting is best?

This is where the two metrics disagree, and the disagreement is the insight.

  • BLEU falls monotonically (1.96 → 0.16). It needs exact n-gram matches against one reference. Greedy's repeated common n-grams accidentally match, so BLEU rewards the degenerate output.
  • chrF++ peaks at T = 1.0 (16.29, and 18.05 for Model L). It scores character n-grams, so it credits correct Devanagari morphology even when the word choice differs from the reference.
Say this

"T = 1.0 removes the repetition and peaks chrF++, so diversity costs no fidelity up to that point. And it shows why BLEU is a poor metric for open-ended generation in a morphologically rich language — a degenerate loop can outscore a fluent continuation."

If they ask where 5.2565 comes from

It is not ln(512) = 6.24. Under causal masking, query position t can only see t+1 keys, so the uniform-attention entropy at that position is ln(t+1). The baseline is the mean of ln(t+1) over all positions, which for T = 512 is 5.2565.

Say this

"Exact match went 0% → 35%, which looks like success. It isn't. On a forced choice between the entities the question actually names, both models are at or below the 41.67% chance rate. Per task it tracks chance almost exactly — 47.4% on the two-candidate families against a 50% chance, 33.4% on three-candidate numeric3 against 33.3%. Why did you add a second metric?

Because exact match on a pretrained model measures the wrong thing. It has never seen the prompt format, so it never emits a bare name, so it scores 0% — a floor artefact, not a measurement. Forced choice scores each named candidate by mean log-probability per answer token and picks the best, so chance is known and the baseline is interpretable.

Length-normalised, deliberately: without it the shortest-tokenizing name wins on total log-probability for reasons unrelated to the question.

"The chain is stated A > B, B > C, so the extremes are always A and C and the middle entity is never correct. Model H answers with the middle entity 72.6% of the time and with A 0.0% of the time. How did you prevent train–test leakage in the synthetic data?

Both defences the brief suggests, applied together, and asserted in code rather than assumed:

  • Held-out entity names — 40 / 10 / 10, disjoint across splits.
  • Held-out templates — 14 / 4 / 4, disjoint across splits.

So a test question is about unseen people phrased in an unseen way. It paid off directly: both models answer test questions with training names — महेश for H, सिर्जना for L. Model L does it 19.2–28.8% of the time against H's 0.0–7.2%.

Say this

"Without disjoint name pools that behaviour would have scored as partial success. It's also the clearest resource-tier difference in the phase: Model H learned to attend to the prompt, Model L only about three-quarters of the way."

Two bugs you caught in your own dataset

Volunteer these. They demonstrate you reviewed your own work adversarially.

1 · A 45% gender-agreement leak

Hindi adjectives inflect. सबसे छोटी कौन है? asked about one woman and two men has exactly one grammatically possible answer — so a model could score by matching agreement without comparing anything. 181 of 400 examples were solvable that way. Fixed by drawing every entity in an example from one gender.

2 · Nepali numerals were unrepresentable

Model L's BPE vocabulary contains no Devanagari digit at all — not one of ०–९ — and it was trained without byte fallback, so every numeral encoded to <unk>, 4.8% of the corpus. Switched Nepali to Arabic digits, which the vocabulary covers completely and which modern Nepali prose uses anyway. Model H's Unigram covers both systems at zero unknowns.

Did finetuning change attention?

Slightly, and in a consistent direction: sharper and longer-range at once.

The methodological point they may probe

"Both checkpoints see the same prompts, all of one token length. Attention entropy depends on what's being attended to, and mean distance and the ln(t) baseline both grow with sequence length — so comparing a 20-token prompt against a 40-token one would show a difference that's purely arithmetic."

Don't overclaim

The shifts are real but small, and accuracy did not improve. The defensible reading: finetuning changed where the model looks without giving it the mechanism to use what it finds. It learned to reach back for a name, but not which name.

Likely follow-ups

Short answers. If you know these, the conversation stays on ground you control.

Rapid fire

Why pre-norm rather than post-norm?
The residual path stays clean — gradients reach layer 0 without passing through a LayerNorm at every step. Post-norm needs careful warmup to train deep stacks at all.
Why tie the output projection to the embedding?
Saves 8,000 × 512 = 4,096,000 parameters, ~15% of Model H, that would otherwise be a second representation of the same vocabulary. Input and output token spaces are the same space.
Why AdamW rather than Adam?
Decoupled weight decay. In Adam, L2 penalty enters the gradient and gets divided by the second-moment estimate, so it is effectively scaled per-parameter. AdamW applies decay directly to the weights. You also decay matrices only — biases, LayerNorm gains and embeddings set scales and offsets rather than doing linear mixing.
Why 6 layers and not 4 wider or 8 narrower?
At n_embd = 512 a block costs ~3.15M parameters; 6 blocks plus embeddings lands at 23.3M, just under the ~25M target — 7 would overshoot to 26.4M. Depth over width because attention needs several rounds of composition to build multi-hop dependencies.
Why gradient accumulation?
batch_size is what fits in VRAM; grad_accum_steps decouples the effective batch from that. 32 × 2 × 512 = 32,768 tokens per optimiser step, identical for both models so token budgets match.
Why fp16 and not bf16 in Phase 3?
The Kaggle T4 is compute-capability 7.5 and has no bfloat16. fp16 needs a GradScaler because its exponent range is narrow enough for gradients to underflow; bf16 does not.
Why is the non-embedding parameter count identical?
Deliberate — 18,915,328 for both. Same depth, width, heads and FFN size. It is what licenses attributing every difference in results to data and tokenizer rather than to capacity.
Why ROUGE-L implemented by hand?
The rouge_score package strips non-ASCII, which zeroes out Devanagari entirely. Yours is an LCS over whitespace tokens.
